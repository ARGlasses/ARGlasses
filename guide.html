AR Glasses Guide: Understand AR, XR & Smart Glasses

AR glasses are not one product category. Some are wearable screens for movies and games, some are everyday AI eyewear, and some are designed to place digital information into the physical world. This guide explains the differences, the specifications that matter and the practical questions to ask before you buy.

02 · KNOW THE CATEGORIES

AR glasses, display glasses and AI glasses are not interchangeable.

Display glasses

Think: wearable monitor. They put a large virtual image in front of you, commonly using micro-OLED panels and optics. They are often connected to a phone, laptop, handheld or console over USB-C. Spatial features may be added through built-in processing or an accessory.

AI smart glasses

Think: connected eyewear. Cameras, microphones, speakers and an AI service can make them useful for calls, capture, translation and assistance. Some have a display; many do not. The phone and cloud ecosystem can be as important as the frame.

Optical / spatial AR

Think: digital information in the environment. Optical see-through systems attempt to keep the real world visible while adding digital content. Tracking, calibration, brightness, field of view and software maturity become especially important.

Enterprise AR

Think: a work instrument. These systems can be built for guided maintenance, logistics, remote assistance, training or visualisation. Ruggedness, management, safety, support and application integration may matter more than consumer entertainment specifications.

XR / immersive display glasses

Think: a portable cinema or workstation. High-resolution panels, wide FOV, 3DoF anchoring and software features can create a more immersive virtual display. They can be excellent for media and productivity without being full optical AR.

Why the labels get confusing

Manufacturers use AR, XR, smart glasses and spatial computing differently. Read the underlying hardware and software claims rather than relying on the category name alone.

03 · DISPLAY & OPTICS

How to read the numbers that shape what you see.

Resolution

“1920 × 1080 per eye” describes the image delivered to each eye. Higher resolution can improve fine detail, text and perceived sharpness, but optics and rendering quality also matter.

Field of view

FOV describes how much of your view the virtual image occupies. A larger figure can make content feel more expansive. Check how the manufacturer defines the measurement before comparing figures.

Brightness

Brightness figures are not always measured in the same way. A manufacturer may quote display brightness or perceived brightness. Outdoor readability also depends on optics, tint and surrounding light.

Refresh rate

60 Hz, 90 Hz and 120 Hz describe how frequently the image can refresh. Maximum figures may depend on resolution, host device, software mode or connection.

Micro-OLED

A compact OLED display technology used widely in modern display glasses. It can deliver high contrast and resolution in a small package, with the optical system determining how that image appears to you.

Waveguides

Waveguide optics can route light from a compact display into the user's view while keeping the glasses relatively thin. Different waveguide architectures have different trade-offs in brightness, colour, FOV and manufacturing complexity.

Important: never choose on brightness, resolution or FOV alone. Two products can publish impressive-looking numbers while delivering very different experiences because their optical architectures, image size, distortion, tint and software are different.

05 · COMPATIBILITY

The glasses are only one part of the system.

Before ordering, check the complete chain: glasses → cable or wireless link → phone, laptop, console or companion device → operating system → app or service. A USB-C connector does not automatically mean every USB-C device can drive a display.

USB-C video

Look for DisplayPort Alt Mode or another explicitly supported video standard. Power and data over USB-C are not the same thing as video output.

Phones & tablets

Check the exact device model, operating system and regional software support. Some spatial features require a companion app or accessory.

PC & Mac

For productivity, verify video output, resolution, refresh-rate support and whether the desired multi-screen or spatial software works on your operating system.

Handhelds & consoles

Steam Deck, ROG Ally, Nintendo Switch and other systems can have different output requirements. Docks and adapters may be necessary, and feature support can vary by model.

Wireless & companion devices

Some glasses depend on a phone, compute puck or dedicated accessory for apps, spatial processing or wireless connectivity. Include that accessory in the total cost and setup.

Prescription lenses

Check the manufacturer's supported prescription range, lens type, regional availability and whether the prescription insert changes fit or optical performance.

06 · EVERYDAY USE

Comfort, privacy and battery can matter more than headline specs.

Weight & balance

Total weight is useful, but distribution matters. A heavier front section can feel different from a slightly heavier frame with better balance.

Battery life

Manufacturer battery claims are usually usage-dependent. Cameras, displays, high brightness, calls, wireless connections and AI processing can all change endurance.

Heat & comfort

Long sessions can reveal pressure points, warmth around the temples or nose and cable-management issues that a short demonstration may not expose.

Camera privacy

If a frame has a camera, check for a visible capture indicator and understand what the companion app and cloud services do with captured information.

Audio leakage

Open-ear and directional speakers are convenient, but sound can still be audible to people nearby. Consider where and how you will use the glasses.

Weather & durability

IP ratings and manufacturer usage guidance vary. Water resistance is not the same as suitability for every outdoor condition or extended exposure.

07 · BEFORE YOU BUY

A practical pre-purchase checklist.

  • Write down the two or three things you will use the glasses for most.
  • Decide whether you need a visible display, audio/AI only, or genuine spatial AR.
  • Confirm your exact phone, laptop, handheld or console is supported.
  • Check resolution, FOV, brightness and refresh rate as a group, not individually.
  • Check whether 3DoF or 6DoF is built in or requires an accessory.
  • Check the real battery claim for your expected usage, not just standby time.
  • Check weight, fit, nose pads, temple size and prescription-lens support.
  • For camera-equipped glasses, read the privacy and capture-indicator information.
  • Confirm what is included in the box and what accessories cost extra.
  • Check current UK pricing, warranty, returns and regional software availability.
  • For work use, check security, device management and application compatibility.
  • Use the manufacturer's current specifications as the final authority when a figure has changed.

08 · GLOSSARY

AR glasses terminology, in plain English.

AR

Augmented reality: digital content presented alongside or within a view of the physical world.

XR

Extended reality: a broad term covering augmented, mixed and virtual-reality experiences.

FOV

Field of view: the angular extent of the visible image or optical display.

3DoF

Three degrees of freedom: rotational tracking around three axes.

6DoF

Six degrees of freedom: rotational tracking plus positional movement through space.

Micro-OLED

A small OLED display technology commonly used in compact wearable displays.

Waveguide

An optical structure that guides light from a display into the user's field of view.

See-through

A design where the user can see the real environment while digital imagery is presented through the optics.

3DoF virtual screen

A display that can appear spatially anchored through rotational tracking without tracking the user's position in the room.
